01

Midmak: one course of a wall

In Arabic a midmak is one course of stone or block in a wall, and laying the first midmak is how a foundation is described. Gaza is being cleared and rebuilt by hand, and West Bank houses stand with their rebar left out for the next floor. The site is built the same way: one course a day.

The block

The three-core hollow block, 40 by 20 cm, that Gaza and the West Bank are built from. It is the hyphen in the name and the unit of every page: one worker, one block.

The line

A mason stretches a string between two pins and lays each block to it. On the site the line marks today, and it is the only live colour.

The bars

Starter bars left standing on a finished roof wait for the next floor. They mark work that is not over, so they stand where tomorrow's course will go.

The marks

Crews spray and chalk walls: a cross, a tick, a strike, a number. State on the site is a mark like these, never a coloured badge.

02

Wordmark

The name is set in Hepta Slab Black, outlined, with a hollow block where the hyphen goes. The block is drawn to the real 2:1 face and rides the middle of the x-height, where a hyphen sits. Keep clear space of one block height on every side.

Construction. Block 1200 × 600 units on a 2000-unit em; three cores, face shells heavier than a real block so the hyphen survives at 16 px. Gaps are optical: 150 units after the l, 128 before the round O.

Three grounds. Charcoal on mortar is the default. On night, the block may take the line colour; on the line, everything is charcoal. Minimum width 120 px on screen, 30 mm in print.

The wall

Layouts are walls in running bond: every course is offset half a block from the one below, so no joint runs through two courses. The grid counts half blocks; a full block spans two, a half block spans one. Joints are 6 px of mortar.

Desktop. Eight halves: four blocks a course.

Tablet. Six halves: three a course.

Today's course is always laid on top, with the line stretched across it; older courses sit below on a darker face, and the footer is the foundation. Starter bars only ever stand at the top edge.

The block

A person is a block: name first, then the trade in plain words, the place, and what they are paid, as they told the reporter. One number may be chalked in the corner when the source gives it: hours worked, times displaced, metres from shore.

08

Marks

Crews mark walls by hand, and the site marks state the same way. A mark is drawn once, in the line colour or oxide, and it carries meaning that colour alone never does, so it also works for readers who do not see colour.

Cross

Closed, sealed, cut off. Crossings and roads.

Tick

Open, paid, back at work.

Strike

A trade someone no longer has. Sprayed when the row arrives.

09

Motion

The site moves the way a wall goes up, and only then. Four motions, each with one job; nothing else animates. Every one of them is skipped when the reader asks for reduced motion, and the page is complete without them.

Snap

The line is stretched once, in the loader: slack, then taut on a damped spring, 6.2 Hz, τ 110 ms.

Lay

Blocks are set course by course as a wall arrives: down 14 px and tapped level, 550 ms, cubic-bezier(.2,.9,.25,1.25), 55 ms apart.

Pluck

Point at a block

The signature. Pointing at or tabbing to a person plucks the line above their course: 8.5 Hz, τ 170 ms, 7 px.

Spray

Nursing graduate → sells internet cards

A struck trade is sprayed once, left to right, 600 ms, when its row comes into view.

Loader. Once per session: the pins land, the line snaps, the block is lowered to it and tapped twice, the name opens out from behind it, and the page is lifted. Any key or click skips it.

10

Social

Most readers meet Pal-Online in a feed, so every story must survive as one card. Four templates cover the edition; each carries its source on the card itself, because a screenshot travels without its link.

Avatars, 1080 px: night for X and Instagram, mortar for Telegram and WhatsApp.

11

Voice

Plain, exact, unhurried. The reader should finish every story knowing a name, a trade and a number, and where the number came from.

A person before a number

Open on someone at work, then widen to the figure.
